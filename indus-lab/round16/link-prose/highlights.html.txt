The cabinet gave up on the figures because the groups “are not separated, by blanks or dots as is usually the case.” The first problem was where one group ends and the next begins. The digits 5 and 8 never occur inside a group, so they are nulls. Without them the stream falls into about sixty two-figure codes. A homophonic solver found nothing. Image: British Library, Harley MS 1582 f. 263r, via DECODE R8505. 05 How these were chosen The thirty come from the two hundred or so write-ups on this site and the notes behind them. They were chosen for the story, and difficulty played no part; the full list sorts by outcome, and the hardest breaks are ranked in the repository’s solved ranking . Each badge names how the text was obtained, in the terms of the site’s glossary. Ciphertext-only break (two of the thirty: Soglia 1848 and D’Ewes 1635) and key from adjacent plaintext (six) mean the key was rebuilt here. Known key applied (eleven) and existing decipherment (three) mean that a key or a decipherment already existed, and the work was transcription and decipherment. Partial means some of the text is still open. Quotations keep the original spelling, and the translations follow the write-ups. Six of the thirty are not ciphers at all, or hold no message; one was deciphered in print long ago, and one is too short to decipher without a key. The six hardest breaks were chosen the other way round, by difficulty alone, from the targets whose key was recovered from the ciphertext alone. The tests: no key, crib or decipherment used; no sibling key; an unknown system; the cipher transcribed from the images here; the reading measured (95% or more of the cipher, except Kauderbach, at 65–97% per letter); and a control, a sibling letter or a re-check on the scans behind the key. Breaks first made by someone else and checked here, such as the Mercy instruction of 1648 (NoAutopilot), are left out, as are four that turned out to be independent re-solutions of a cipher someone had already broken. Two of the six, the Mémoire of 1560 and the Ségur letters, had been solved privately by George Lasry, who never published them; the cards say so.
